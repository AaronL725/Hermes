--- 帖子 #2 ---
I will note: rules can be broken. The more adaptable and proficient you are, the more likely it is that you will be comfortable with breaking rules. But, here are some explanations, rules, and general principles. An Iron Condor is a spread trade. Basically, you are selling an option and buying another further out option at the same expiration date. If you do that with calls only, you'll have a short call vertical spread, or a bear call vertical. If you do the same thing with puts, at the same time, you'll be adding a bull put vertical, and then you'll have an Iron Condor. The tasty standard (and there are variations) is to enter these vertical spreads @ 45 days to expiration (45 dte). Typically, the short options (the guts) are at 20 delta, and the wings are $5, $10, or $20 beyond that. Short verticals are put on for a credit. The tasty standard for managing the trades is to take off an unchallenged leg of the trade when it reaches 50% of max profit, and to roll a challenged leg when the short strike is breached, or at 21 dte, whichever comes first. So, if you get a $10 credit when you put on the trade, and after some time you buy it back for $5, then you made $5 profit ($500 on a 1-lot). If the underlying price moves away from the spread, it will decrease in value. The options in the spread will also decrease in value through time decay (theta). I'll show how to roll a spread in due time, by example. Note, SPX is the preferred vehicle, but you can use any options to set up an IC. I like European style option SPX because it's large, liquid, has daily expiries, and is cash settled. XSP is another euro-style option that has 10% the size of SPX. (it's the "micro" SPX)...XSP volume is kinda thin, but it's tradeable. If you're inexperienced with these but want to try trading them live, I'd recommend using 1-lot XSP....

--- 帖子 #4 ---
anyone trading options should acquire a good understanding of options Greeks. Especially germane for selling options is theta. A simple definition of theta is: the rate of decline in an option's value due to the passage of time. Aka "time decay". Time decay is an options seller's friend. Generally, if you are selling ICs, you want the underlying to be range bound while time passes. If you are just selling one side, say a short vertical call spread, then you want the underlaying price to move away from the spread strikes. That's why we call it a "bear" call vertical - it's a bearish trade. The Feb05 SPX 4950/4500 bear calls trade above - the 4950 call has theta = -0.63 - this means that, all else being constant, the value of the 4950 call will decrease by $0.63 per day, &amp; $0.45 per day for the 5000 call. "All else being constant' is a big "if", because everything is changing, always. Nevertheless, keep an eye on theta and understand how it changes over time and relative to the other important parameters that go into figuring an option's price. Attached Image (click to enlarge)

GOOD question. One thing most people agree on is, if price is moving against the calls, then you roll up the put spread for additional credit. What you can do to the call side (in a hard move against the calls) is variable. It kind of depends on how you feel about the original premise of the trade. In the SPX IC above, I have been caught in my assumption that the upmove in SPX would play out and SPX pa would level out. The indices have been seen to do this frequently - put on step-wise upmoves - big move over a few days, followed by weeks of relatively sideways action. However, it looks like the market is primed to keep melting up. I will wait til this heavy fundi week is done by Thurs/Fri to see what I may do. I could roll the calls up and out a couple weeks, do the same to the puts, and reduce my loss. (When I say loss I mean loss against the original $8 credit of the trade.) Or, I could take the loss sooner than later if it seems I will be fighting a runaway bull market. Continuously rolling in a melt-up can drag out for weeks, is exhausting. The call spreads are a lot richer than the put spreads, so rolling up &amp; out multiple times will inevitably erode the credit down towards $0. The best time to trade IC's is when you expect the market to not move much - I knew it was a gamble putting this one on thru an FOMC and earnings, something I will remember better next time if I suffer thru this one.
