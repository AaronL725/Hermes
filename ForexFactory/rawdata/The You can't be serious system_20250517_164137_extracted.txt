Some basic principles I know you can't wait for the system revelation, but you should really go through some basic principles before. I won’t talk about how to build a systematic model or algos. If you want to know more about what I think of these topics and you have a masochistic tendency, you can read my long winded blurb here: https://www.forexfactory.com/thread/...trading-debate ? But I get it: people do not like long posts, or explanations (so last century!) so, I will try to do a very quick, dirty, deeply unsatisfactory and unprofessional summary (so this century!) here: Dirty and simplistic list part 1: 1) Rules are good, you need rules for many reasons and rules should adhere to some basic, sound principles 2) Rules can be tested and stats can be applied 3) The more mechanical the rules the more reliable stats are 4) Totally mechanical rules could be automated so you may go into algorithmic systems Flip side, dirty and simplistic list part 2: 5) No fun, no education, no "merit" in using algo. Plus, some rules are too difficult to code 6) Automated systems are usually a short blanket: they may keep your vital organs warm but you will always be uncomfortable. 7) Use more "fun", "merit-based", experience based, educated "filters" to "correct" the sterile automatic rules BUT!! Point 7 implies a number of costs: a) Time: you have to be in front of your charts to make the decisions b) Time and money: you have to build your knowledge and experience c) Less diversification:" a)" implies you have to focus on fewer markets. Less diversification equals more risk d) There is no guarantee you can predict the market better than the automatic rules and it is much more difficult to assess if you actually do. e) Probably the highest cost of all: our human biases are highly detrimental in taking financial and trading decision, as behavioural finance, prospect theory and the lot have widely demonstrated. Psychological biases can be and usually are our worst enemy. Each of the points above would deserve a thread on its own, but let's stop here. In this thread we will focus, in a very partial way, on 1) and on 7) One more point, and another crucial one. Most of the content in these forums are dealing with these issues. If you go through some of the threads here you will find many systems and methods, with all the variants you like. Many people say they work, many say they don't, many change or add rules. That is because we are all different. It is vital to understand what kind of person/trader you are: impulsive, analytical, contrarian, trend follower, break-outer, mean-reverter, disciplined, disorganised, fearful, fearless, etc. There is no such thing as "the winning system" without pairing it with what kind of trader you are. The "you can’t be serious" system will probably follow the same fate. But in doing so perhaps something will stick to some people, prompt them to look further, or re-consider their options and this may help them. I will do my best to keep this thread as a "system thread" and not to transform it into a course.

What makes sense and, above all, has been proven to work? It is widely accepted that, for example, trend following fits the bill. Mean reversion may too, break-out from ranges could be another, or various examples of carry trades. Some of you will like some more than others. Hi All, No. Not " almost there ". Exactly there A mathematical analysis by linear regression shows that the histogram is NOT generated by just the slope of the MA. It is precisely generated by the cross of the MA with another 5-period MA of itself which is the signal line. You can reduce this via linear regression to the coefficients of a FIR (finite impulse response) filter for the SMA and those of an IIR (infinite impulse response) filter for the EMA. The mathematics does not lie. I have been at this game for far too many years to be easily fooled... I am sure if you publish the MQL4 you will see what is going on.... KR, OD .

I will follow up on point 3 above: Other levels that you can “easily” identify from your chart (the emphasis is on “easily”.) This is where we start introducing discretion, and where all the problems linked with it start. 1) Pivots and BRN are quite easy to plot and rather non discretional (hint, hint: they are easy to code into the strategy) 2) There are other easily identifiable points: Daily open, Yesterday Close, etc. I will omit them for now for simplicity, but if you like them, you can use them. 3) Some people may want to push it further and claim that they can “identify the points where there are signs of tensions between buyers and sellers” via Fibonacci levels, or Eliot wave or whatever. If you love them and you want to use them, do it. However, it is much more subjective to decide when to measure a Fib or a wave sequence from than it is to tell where the market opened today. The level of discretion is high. So, I would not use them, but you do what you want. 4) The same goes for the more classical Technical analysis (TA) tools. Where you draw your trendlines from is relatively subjective. Having said that, TL could be quite effective, as are channels and so on. Too much to discuss. Let's omit them for now. So, why on earth should we use “Other levels that you can “easily” identify from your chart”? Because I think they make a great job at telling you were support and resistance have been in the past. In this example, we are far away from Pivots or BRN, but it should be pretty clear that there are no seller below that green line and that buyers are defending their positions very strongly there. Not only, when sellers finally break that level, buyers counter-attack and push sellers up, but seller are defending that same level now: “support becomes resistance” Attached Image (click to enlarge) Or, adding to the GY chart we used before, "today’s" S1 held a bit but then was broken, only to find support at "yesterday’s" support level (the green level at the bottom of the chart) Attached Image (click to enlarge) Finding these levels is a bit of an art in itself and one can use many different techniques to identify them with more accuracy (volume could be a very powerful tool in this sense). Let's keep it easy here and focus on what our eyes could detect. It needs practice and training. That is up you!

This version of the strategy looks at the bar that gave the signal and places a buy order a few points (it depends on the instrument) above the high. What about the “filtered” results? They look good, don't they? I think it is because they are based on additional rules that make sense, are tested and seem to work. However, as we know, they have a price. Digging a bit in the SMS results, we can notice that the MAs slopes are way too slow. The “let your profit run” principle implies that you have to accept big pull backs. Several times some good runs have given back much of the profit because the MAs slopes were lagging too much. Also when big, sudden spikes occur, especially during big news but not only, the signal is whiplashed long/short and trades are caught completely off guard. This has the effect of creating rather big losses 2 or 3 times in a row in a situation where at the end the market barely moved. It is a typical “flaw” of trend following techniques. All this leads to the thought that trend following strategies like this simple one may perhaps do a decent job at finding entries. But they are pretty poor at finding exits. Here we open another can of worms. Many developers are adamant that having different rules for entry and exit is wrong. They have a point. The interference between conflicting rules creates big coding problems, impacts the rules themselves and they can’t be properly measured anymore. Moreover, it sounds a lot like rules fitting, which is a very delicate topic. Another big problem with this approach is that we will abandon the “always in” system. This implies, as I mentioned, a huge review of the risk management aspects. And we need to ask what are we going to use instead. The easiest answer is usually the fixed fractional method. Perhaps one day will go into more detail, but if you can't wait, the web is full of talks on this point, including this forum. Another massive issue is that exit rules are usually more difficult to code than entry rules. This leads us once again to the concept of discretionarily applied rules. Ah, the wonderful topic of false break-out! How could I possibly explain in a few words? We know I can’t and if I try I will rapidly become the most boring and least popular of all the posters here! But I start from an already very low level, so what do I have to lose? I could answer in a tweet-like way and say “it depends”. I’d rather answer in a typical intellectual and pedantic way and start with asking: when do we define a break-out as false? I will try to focus on some thoughts that may be of interest. Attached Image (click to enlarge) It is a very subjective issue. Sometimes it is easy to define. B is definitely a BO. Some text books say that we have a good BO when we reach our target before we hit our stop loss (SL) or when we are in profit once we hit our SL (trailing.) it is a good start. We could say that the lows never re-test the entry point. The sellers are on the run chased by the bulls and they do not really defend their positions. We could also say with some confidence that A is false break-out. However, this case is more complicated. It is, according to our parameters. We set the short entry at that level (1) for whatever reason and that level was broken. We define it false because it reached our SL BEFORE it reached our target. Had the short entry order been a few points below that level we would not be talking about a BO at all, false or otherwise. So, what is a false BO (or even just a BO) for you could be such just for you, depending on your definition and parameters. That may not satisfy your curiosity, so let me expand. What does it take to tell you that a BO is not really a good BO (or it is a false BO)? Where you place the SL is crucial from this point of view. If you, for some strange reason, had put the the SL just above the bar that generated the BO, you would call this a “false BO" at the bar immediately after you entered. If you chose your SL "more properly" you would have put it at the red line, above the double top that started a sideways move. You would not have called this a "false BO" until much much further in the day. Notice also how the point where you would have ben stopped out may well constitute a BO in the other direction. Was that a "good BO"? Difficult to say until B. You could have been still in by the time it reached B. Or you could have tighten the stop and have another "false BO" on the doji just before B! But let's focus on A. Some traders want to see a clear momentum following the BO (like in B). If they do not see it, they bail out at breakeven and look for another opportunity. For example, you entered at 1. That BO was immediately rejected (see the next bar). But as you set your SL above the Double top (red line) that is not a false BO for you, not yet at least. So, you are still in and now the sellers are trying to push the market down. They had about 6 or 7 attempts to force the market down. There is obviously no great momentum behind this BO and some traders will prefer not to risk and wait for a better opportunity. We did not lose any money, but it is still a false BO, of some sort. Confused? Welcome to the club!

Going back to your actual question, on the tighter SL placement, I am not sure I understand, but that is because it depends on what kind of risk management you use. This is a good opportunity to talk a bit about risk and trade management. I apologise if you already know these concepts, but it may be useful for less experienced traders. If you use a fixed fractional risk (say 1% of account), it would not really matter (in some ways) what option you use, as with a tight stop you can trade more lots. Trivialising, if your 1% is 100$ and your SL is, say, 20 points (let's say option 1), you would trade 5 lots. If the SL is 50 points (let's say option 2) you would trade 2 lots. You are always risking 100$, which is 1%. This may become more relevant in conjunction with your trade management. If your strategy is to take (partial or total) profit at, say 1:1 RR, you will "only" need 20 points to achieve that with option 1, but you will need 50 points with option 2. So, is option 1 better? Not necessarily, as the trade can go against you and you have only 20 points before you have a loss with option 1, where you have a much bigger breathing space of 50 points with option 2. the risk is 100$ in both cases. It is, as it always is, a trade off. Apologies if it this sounds like stating the obvious for most of you.

Unfortunately with one rule to get in and out this happens frequently. Incidentally, this is one of the reasons why it is much easier to focus on entries than on exits, and why many, many strategies are good at entries but suck at exits. But this is a much more complex topic. The natural solution would be to take profit at some points. As always, in trading as in life, you have to compromise. Taking profit means, by definition, going against the old wisdom “let your profit run”. So by taking profit you are giving up the possibility of being in the market if the trend continues. There are several solutions to this: taking profit on only a part of your trade, or playing with re-entries, for example. Keep in mind that for every solution you create a new compromise. You have to live with that notion.

Instrument choice (focused on FX, here) What follows is a “brief” note on some ground rules that traders should be aware of when choosing pairs. Oversimplified as usual. Apologies to the expert traders. The most important traits I am looking for in a pair are: volatility, liquidity and correlation. Volatility because we trade a momentum/breakout system. Liquidity because I need low spread (remember: spreads are costs, and the lower your timeframes, usually the higher your trading frequency, so, the higher your costs.) Correlation …..where do I start? So, simply put, I look at the average excursion (range) in pips (ADR, or ATR, or whatever: you pick yours) and the lowest spread and then I check the correlation. This is usually a compromise. For example, EU and UY are extremely liquid, but they are way down the volatility list. Why is this important? Because, in systems like this, having an instrument that on average moves 100 points is better than having one that moves 30. Depending on the risk management method you use, it may seem to make no difference: if you use fixed fractional it is always (say) 1%. Right? It is a bit trickier than that. I do not have time here to go deep into this, but think about, for instance, broker’’s fees. 1% risk is 1% risk, but if on one instrument you can “only” trade 5 lots and on another you can trade 15 lots, other things equal, the latter is 3 times more expensive. Let’s stop here for now. Once you have identified a bunch of suitable instruments, you have to analyse their correlation. The importance of correlation would deserve several chapters in any trading book. I won’t try it here . Let’s look at an example. According to one measurement of volatility (and ignoring liquidity,) in one of my tables I have these pairs on top: GBPNZD, GBPAUD, EURNZD, GBPCAD, GBPJPY. Notice anything peculiar? Yeup! GBP is in almost all of them. Now if you were using these instruments, you would have an exposure to GBP that is multiplied by 4 (unless you divide the regular risk on these pair by four!) If the Governor of the BoE comes out with a “strange statement” and the GBP moves a lot, 4 of your 5 pairs will be going in exactly the same direction. This makes no sense in risk management terms. Another thing to remember is that the higher the time frame, the higher the correlation. For example, on Daily charts AUDUSD and NZDUSD have a correlation of 94 (they are basically the “same thing”.) If you look at the 15m correlation, it is 74. Still very high, but a quarter less. It is even less on 5 and 1m. So, let’s have a look at “my 3 pairs”: GU/UC have a correlation of -58 on 15 m and -10 on 1H, GU/EA is 7 for 15m and 58 for 1H, UC/EA is -20 for 15m and 5 for 1H. You can do worse than this. This is just scraping the surface, as usual. I am sorry to complicate things further, but I can’t resist! For the more advanced traders, I should add that there are situations where using correlated pairs may make sense.
