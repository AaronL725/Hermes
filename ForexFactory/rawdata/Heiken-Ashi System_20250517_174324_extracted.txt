For anybody interested, here is what the Heiken_Ashi_Smoothed.mq4 indicator does. The parameters are: MA Method &amp; Period (period=number of bars) for moving average #1 MA Method &amp; Period for moving average #2 Valid MA methods are: 0 = SMA (simple) 1 = EMA (exponential) 2 = SMMA (smoothed) 3 = LWMA (linear weighted) Thus valid values for MAMetod and MAMetod2 are 0, 1, 2 or 3. 1. Using MA method/period #1, the formula calculates separate moving averages for the Open, High, Low and Close of the current bar. 2. Heiken-Ashi values are then calculated for the values returned in step 1. Formula for H-A is: haClose = (Open + High + Low + Close) / 4 haOpen = (haOpen(previous bar) + haClose(previous bar))/2 haHigh = Maximum(High, haOpen) haLow = Minimum(Low, haOpen) 3. Using MA method/period #2, the formula calculates separate moving averages for the O, H, L and C of the values returned in step 2. These values are plotted on the chart. Given that Heiken-Ashi is a form of averaging, there are three levels of smoothing taking place, effectively creating a “triple lag”. The parameters recommended by the author are: MAMetod = 2 MAPeriod = 6 MAMetod2 = 3 MAPeriod2 = 2 (Note: these can’t be 2, 3, 6, 2 because 6 is an invalid MA method) So step 1 is a SMMA(6) of OHLC; step 2 is taking the HA values; step 3 is further applying a LWMA(2). Incidentally, if the parameters were entered as 0, 1, 0, 1 this would mean taking an SMA(1) at both steps 1 &amp; 3, hence effectively removing them from the calculation (since a 1 period MA of any value = the value itself). Thus we would be left with standard H-A candles (academic interest only, I'm not suggesting that this is a profitable method). David

The Fibo method is less risky than the Martingale in the sense that the steps ascend more slowly. For example, the twentieth Fibo number is 6,765 while 2^19 = 524,288, which differs by a significant order of magnitude. But the Fibo method apparently requires two successive wins to return to breakeven; the Martingale only one. This is the kind of compromise that underlies any money management (MM) system, i.e. probability is in some way inversely proportional to payoff size. Casino games like Roulette offer a reasonable analogy. Just like with Roulette, I don’t believe it’s possible to beat the markets statistically using only MM . To be assured of winning with long term consistency, the method of entry/exit (in this case Heiken-Ashi) has to deliver an implicit positive expectancy. In the same way, the casino always eventually wins at Roulette because the game’s expectancy is significantly (5.26%) in the casino’s favor. MM is irrelevant. With any MM system of increasing bet size, the point will eventually be reached where the number of successive losses exceeds one’s asset base, even if one is Bill Gates. The probability of attaining a prolonged losing sequence is higher than most people imagine. The following table illustrates this. The left column shows the percentage of winning trades (‘batting average’), the topmost row the number of successive losses. The intersection in the body of the table shows the probability of incurring this number of losses in a sequence of 50 trials (trades). For example, if one’s system has a win rate of 55%, then the probability of incurring 6 successive losses anywhere within a 50 trade sequence is as high as 31%. All values are rounded to the nearest percent, e.g. 100% doesn’t mean absolute certainty, just that the value exceeds 99.5%. The table assumes a normal probability distribution. However, I’ve read that with trading, sequences of wins and losses tend to 'cluster' together, while market conditions that are either favorable, or unfavorable, to one’s system, remain in force. Hence the probabilities are likely to be even higher than the values in the table. David [Edit: The table shows the probability for N successive losses. In the case of the Fibo MM system, two successive wins are needed to attain breakeven. Hence (for example) 1 win embedded anywhere in 7 losing trades, or 2 non-successive wins embeeded anywhere within 8 losing trades, etc brings one no closer to breakeven than 6 consecutive losses. There are hundreds of possible combinations of achieving these outcomes. Hence the Fibo system has a significant barrier to overcome. Another very important consideration is that MM systems where bet size is increased are incredibly difficult to stomach. When the stakes increase, desperation and panic start to set in, resulting in irresistible temptation to compromise the entry/exit rules. This is the complete antithesis of a prudent approach to trading. Like any trading system, this system will ultimately stand or fail on how well or badly the Heiken-Ashi method exploits non-random price behavior, in generating its entry and exit signals. If it can deliver a profit factor = (number of wins x average net win size) / (number of losses x average net loss size) that is &gt; 1, on balance, across all market conditions, then it has the hallmarks of a profitable system.]

Consider a system with 50% win expectancy (e.g. a coin toss, where "heads" represents a win). Let's bet using the Martingale across 3 trials, i.e. start with an initial stake of 1, and double every time a loss is encountered. With 3 trials, there are 2^3 = 8 possible outcomes, shown in the rows of each table in the first attachment. The upper table shows the results of using the Martingale system; the lower table the result of using a fixed size betting (stake = 1 in every trial). Notice how the total expectancy of both systems is exactly zero . We could try the experiment with a larger number of trials, but the result will still be zero in both cases. The key point is that MM has no effect on expectancy . The second attachment repeats the calculations for a system with a 60% win rate. The Martingale appears to deliver a superior result, but that is because the win bias is being applied across an average higher bet size (i.e. a greater amount of one's total capital is being staked, on average. Equates to greater return, and greater risk, in direct proportion). We could equalize by placing a fixed bet of size 1.5 in the lower table. Then both tables would deliver a total expectancy of 3.6. The fact that the expectancy is positive (60%) is what is generating the overall positive outcome, the MM (average bet size) is simply magnifying the result. The third attachment repeats the calculations for a system with a 80% win rate, and illustrates much the same point. The fourth attachment repeats the calculations for a system with a 40% win rate. Note how the expectancy is negative, and is again magnified by the higher average bet size of the Martingale. If we transplant this analogy into a trading setting, it is the profit factor = (number of wins x ave net win size) / ( number of losses x ave net loss size ) generated by the entry/exit system that determines whether or not a system will be mathematically profitable in the long term. A PF of &gt; 1 results in an upward sloping equity curve; a PF &lt; 1 results in an equity curve that slopes downward to eventual bankruptcy. If we compile a frequency distribution of the wins and losses, its standard deviation determines the deviation from an average equity curve, and thus is proportional to expected drawdown. In other words, the greater the deviation, the greater the worst case drawdown is likely to be. The MM (position sizing method) simply scales (magnifies or diminishes) both the slope of the equity curve, and the deviation, i.e. return and risk in direct proportion. David

The attached ZIP contains an XLS that allows you to experiment with different betting systems across 6 trials (trades), where you set the win rate of the system. The yellow background cells are the ones that require your input. These are currently set to a Martingale betting system, i.e. After a win, multiply the prior bet size by 0 and add 1 (sets it back to 1) After a loss, multiply the prior bet size by 2 and add 0 (doubles it) You can try anti-Martingale systems, where you increase bet size following a win (instead of a loss), to try to exploit winning streaks. There are 2^6 = 64 possible outcomes. The final expectancy of each possible outcome is shown in the rightmost column of the chart. The overall results are shown at top right: a) Total overall expectancy: given win rate, and betting system, this is the expected effect on your bank balance (on average) at the end of the 6 trials b) Average bet size: the average bet size across all possible outcomes c) Expectancy divided by ave bet size is (a) divided by (b). This gives you a realistic measure as to whether your betting system is enhancing overall expectancy d) Percentage of positive outcomes: the number of positive results (out of 64 outcomes). If this value is high, then it suggests that you will get a positive outcome more frequently, but the average positive outcome will be lower (relative to the negative outcomes). And vice versa. The trick is to try to increase the value of (c), but without increasing the win rate. Hopefully this will convince you that it is the win rate of the entry/exit system (actually the profit factor, because we also need to take win and loss sizes into account) that determines overall expectancy, and that MM is irrelevant. Note that the model here is likely imperfect, in that it assumes a normal frequency distribution based on the entered win rate, whereas when trading, wins might tend to "cluster" together while market conditions remain favorable, and losses cluster when unfavorable. If this can be shown to be the case, then an anti-Martingale type system might enhance overall profitability. Furthermore, to whatever extent it is possible to grade the probability of entry setups, then IMHO one is justified in increasing position size with the setups that hold greatest probability of profit. The Heiken-Ashi (smoothed) indicator looks quite nice and I have found it helpful in conjunction with my other indicator. But as Hanover points out, the HA(S) as written introduces quite a bit of lag due to the moving averages used. One can do much better than the conventional indicator by using Ultra-Low-Lag smoothers such as the one in my thread "Optimised Trend Trading". This ULL-MA has a smoothing equivalent to a 40-bar MA yet with a lag of around 0.3 bar. While overshoot and price fidelity are slightly poorer than the Jurik JMA, the ULL-MA has much less lag than JMA and is also smoother than JMA -- these latter characteristics are all that matters for trading!! So I use the ULL-MA to initially smooth the OHLC streams, then apply the HA calculations, then smooth again with the ULL-MA. Total lag &lt;1 bar. One could actually just trade this indicator alone, but like all MA systems, one can get whipped in ranging markets. So just combine this with your favourite trend/non-trend detection indicator and you have a good system.
