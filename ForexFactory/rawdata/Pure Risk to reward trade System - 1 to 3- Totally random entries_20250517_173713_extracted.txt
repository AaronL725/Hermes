Hi, I want to start off by saying that I mean no disrespect to anyone here, and that I truly believe you have genuine faith in idea that the risk to reward ratio is a factor in profitable trading. I'm just here to debunk the idea in general. While it is true that "RRR" is an important factor to note, I find it always annoying when people tout it like they understand why it's important. I'm not sure who started it, but I smirk every time someone talks about "RRR" here, because the way it's stated is almost always misleading, as in the case here. The truth is, "RRR" is an important factor for a different reason than what is being "taught" in this forum generally. The misinformation is almost malicious, because it gives false confidence where none is warranted. A little basic math will perhaps clear the misunderstanding: EV, or expected value is the determining factor for judging if a strategy/trade is profitable. This is a fact. No amount of fancy wordplay will change it. An EV&gt;0 is profitable, EV&lt;0 is unprofitable. The EV of a particular trade for example, is calculated as (simplified) the probability of it hitting TP*reward - probability of hitting SL*risk. As you can see, the "RRR" does indeed play a part in the calculation, but it is but one part of the equation. Now, if we do some substitution, we will see why this entire experiment is futile. In this experiment, we assume the markets movements and our actions are completely random. That would mean that the probability of hitting TP,P(tp) is 1/3 the probability of hitting SL,P(sl). EV = P(tp)*reward-P(sl)*risk if P(tp) = x, and P(sl)=3*P(tp) EV = x*reward-3*x*risk but reward = 3*risk (according to the RRR). EV = x*(3*risk) - 3*x*risk EV = 0 That's IF there is 0 spread and 0 commission. In other words, your experiment here is one of variance, not EV, and really proves nothing more than the fact that the variance will exist. RRR in most cases should not even be used as a variable for consideration when determining profitable strategies. We usually use it to estimate our variance and manage drawdown.

Sorry TEB63 but this sytem (or any of its kind) will always fail. Let me explain why. 1) The RRR rules makes failure a near certain outcome ---------------------------------------------------- You assume a winning trade of 158 pips has the same likelihood of occurring as a losing trade of 50 pips. Hence you define "random" as having the same chance of picking a winner vs a loser. This is a fatal flaw. That might be true if you were to assume an equal chance of a trade gaining 50 pips or losing 52 pips (accounting for the spread). As soon as you say I have to get 150+ pips on one side versus 50 pips on the other side, the shorter side will win a vast majority of the time. In this instance your RRR rule undermines any successful outcome. A RRR rule when applied randomly will NOT increase your chance of gaining pips but instead decreases your chance. 2) The Money Management Rules do not help -------------------------------------------- Money management does not increase odds of winning any given trade. Money management is to limit loss and risk exposure. You define Money Management as only risking 1% of your account. That is not money management that is just slowing the rate of loss (and conversely the rate of gain). 3) Not all Currencies Pairs are equal ----------------------------------- You do not account for the range of price fluctuation differences between various currency pairs. I do not trade the AUD/USD because its Average Daily Range is a low 106 pips as opposed to the EUR/JPY with an ADR of 208 pips. A trade in AUD/USD is less likely to gain 150 pips over a course of a week than is a trade in EUR/JPY. On the other hand a stop loss of 50 pips will get triggered more quickly and more certainly in the EUR/JPY than in AUD/USD. Though you select currencies randomly, there is not an equal chance between different pairs of hitting your target or getting stopped for a loss. In conclusion, your results are just about what I would have expected. I wanted to further explain technical analysis versus fundamental analysis and why both can and should work together to increase the odds of a winning trade or series of trades.
