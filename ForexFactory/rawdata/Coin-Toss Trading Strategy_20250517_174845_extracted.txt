martingale Wikipedia Originally, martingale referred to a class of betting strategies popular in 18th century France . The simplest of these strategies was designed for a game in which the gambler wins his stake if a coin comes up heads and loses it if the coin comes up tails. The strategy had the gambler double his bet after every loss, so that the first win would recover all previous losses plus win a profit equal to the original stake. Since a gambler with infinite wealth will with probability 1 eventually flip heads, the Martingale betting strategy was seen as a sure thing by those who practised it. Unfortunately, none of these practitioners in fact possessed infinite wealth, and the exponential growth of the bets would eventually bankrupt those foolish enough to use the Martingale. Moreover, it has become impossible to implement in modern casinos, due to the betting limit at the tables. Because the betting limits reduce the casino's short term variance, the martingale system itself does not pose a threat to the casino, and many will encourage its use, knowing that they have the house advantage no matter when or how much is wagered Anti-Martingale In a classic martingale betting style, gamblers will increase their bets after each loss in hopes that an eventual win will recover all previous losses. The anti-martingale approach instead increases bets after wins, while reducing them after a loss. The perception is that in this manner the gambler will benefit from a winning streak or a "hot hand", while reducing losses while "cold" or otherwise having a losing streak. This general idea of increasing bets when conditions are believed to be favorable can improve the odds in games with a memory by using a strategy like card counting . But in a true random memoryless game there is no such thing as a winning streak or losing streak (these notions are gambler's fallacy ) so this strategy can't improve the expected winnings in such situations. One activity where money management based on an anti-martingale approach has a recognized value [1] is speculation and trading . Many financial markets have some cyclical component to them, and the approach of an individual speculator or trader may only be appropriate for one portion of that cycle. Using an anti-martingale risk management scheme will increase profits during time periods when a trading approach is working well, while automatically decreasing exposure during portions of the cycle where trading is unprofitable. This is believed to decrease the risk of ruin for trading.

This is 100% correct. Adjustment in bet (position) size can only be justified in an event where expectancy can be shown to likewise greater. (That is how the Blackjack pros in the 1970s/80s beat the casinos: placing larger bets when the card count was highly positive, i.e. higher number of favorable cards remaining to be dealt). If (and I stress IF) it can be shown that wins and losses tend to "cluster" together, i.e. a winning sequence improves the probability that the next trade will also be a win, only then is an increase in position size justified. Position sizing systems (e.g. martingale, anti-martingale) do not, in themselves, alter expectancy. Any reliable authority on games involving probability will confirm all of this. If exit (as opposed to position size) is what is meant by MM, then I submit the following theories for consideration: a) The principle of cutting losses quickly, and letting profits run, will complement a trend following entry method. This will generally result in a win rate of less than 50%, but there will be occasional large wins to compensate. The more the market "trends", the greater this effectiveness of this approach. b) Using back-testing to optimize TPs and trailing SL values amounts to curve-fitting in much the same way that optimizing indicators used for entry does. To what extent, if any, this can provide a long term edge is open to debate (and has been debated regularly on the forum). c) Whatever "randomness" might exist with entries also exists with exits, since the market cares nothing as to whether an individual trader is entering or exiting. If "high probability" entry is about finding long term inefficiencies, then exits can potentially improve overall expectancy by likewise exploiting inefficiencies. If random entries/calculated exits can be shown to be profitable, then calculated entries/random exits must necessarily be equally so...... d) ...... albeit with one proviso. Exit doubles as a function of risk management (capital preservation). However, I see no reason why SLs can’t first be set according to technical considerations, and then position sizes set to control the amount at risk. Must admit that I haven’t seen the BES or the Daily Stochastics. David

Spot on, IMHO, Bill. Everything else being equal, a wider TP (relative to SL) means good RR (win size), but low win rate. A wider SL vice versa. There's a widely held belief that cutting losses quickly and letting profits run is enough to give one a discernable edge. I've yet to either prove it or disprove it. It's always good to see people trying to think innovatively, like many of the posters in this thread. When I first started, I likewise thought I had a good chance at conquering the markets by way of mathematical sleight-of-hand. But alas, it's not that simple. If there was a clear-cut "Holy Grail" trick or technique, everybody would be using it, and eventually there wouldn't be a market, since each transaction must ultimately involve a willing buyer and a willing seller. There's an excellent post by Plutonite here: http://www.forexfactory.com/showthread.php?p=1810347 Here's a section of it: "....... It is a MIRACLE that we are making ANY correct statements about a market that was at one point thought to be inherently chaotic and foolish to participate in. Remember that it took several academic papers in the Journal of Finance to prove that the market is not a random walk model. In fact, the market (and any similar exchange) is inherently chaotic, due to the large number of unrelated participants whose actions both commercial and speculative cannot be previously determined, and whose collective effect, weighed by volume, is even harder to predict. It's a crazy game we are playing if you think about it. Just because you have charts that seem to tell a picture in hindsight does not mean that anything makes sense at all. This is an unknown function with a built in random factor, and we're trying to ride it. F*cking crazy. At the same time, there seem to be recurring patterns in the market activity that repeat themselves , and which do form both a statistical and an intuitive basis to make time series propositions (aka trades), but that is a topic for another discussion. ......." IMHO, that sums it up. Somehow the trader must find a phenomenon, or a pattern, amongst the chaos, that offers a potentially demonstrable edge, and then devise a method of entries and exits that systematically exploits it. The function of position size can only be simple. In a positive expectancy situation, the key is to keep bet size small (and consistent with expectancy), allowing the account to survive a series of losses, and giving expectancy the opportunity to ultimately prevail. In a negative expectancy situation, the best hope mathematically is to place one's entire bankroll on a single lucky shot, and then quit while (in reality, if) one is ahead. IMHO, scaling into, and/or out of, trades appears sophisticated, but each component trade still has to deliver, on balance, in its own right. Otherwise it is worthless. For example, it might seem smart to take partial profit and move SL to breakeven, at a predetermined point, guaranteeing a winner. But that means that only half of the trade has the potential to achieve a "home run", while the whole position carries the risk of an early stop out. Again, everything else being equal, the innovative MM delivers no long term mathematical benefit: to decrease risk, we are decreasing potential return.
