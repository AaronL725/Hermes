IMHO, methods can work profitably for two possible reasons: 1. They are respected and used by enough influential traders ("big dogs"), i.e. if enough of these folk believe that a reversal is likely to occur at a certain point, they will place their orders accordingly, effectively causing the reversal to occur (aka the "self-fulfilling prophecy" concept). 2. They somehow otherwise manage to capture underlying crowd psychology. The Forex market consists of a diverse number of participants, some with speculative goals, while others (from multinationals to private individuals) are simply seeking to move money between countries, for whatever reason. Because any number of participants may be in the market at any time, and we have no way of knowing their motives, price movement will always appear to have a certain element of randomness about it, even if that’s not actually the case. I would expect that line studies (prior S/R, Fibo retracements, trendlines, round numbers, etc) have a better chance of meeting the self-fulfilling prophecy idea, than either candle patterns or indicators. Here’s why. Line studies are less vulnerable to timeframe variations, and they don’t take parameters. If price makes a local high on a 4 hourly chart, it will also appear as a high on the relevant hourly, 30 minute, 15 minute, etc bars also. Same logic applies for any Fibo retracement. A round number (e.g. 2.0000) is of course going to be same on every chart. So these are likely to be visible to, and hence used by, a wider number of traders. Fibo retracements depend only on where the anchor points (0.0, 100.0) are set. Daily floor trader pivots (PP, S1, R1, etc) are going to be consistent to the extent that traders use the same cut-off point (e.g. midnight New York EST). Candle patterns (e.g. pin or inside bars) on a 4 hour chart are time zone dependent. Four hour patterns that use GMT+1 as a base are going to be different to those that use GMT+2 as a base, and so on, because they’re gathering different data to build the candle. Indicators are potentially the most vulnerable of all because: a) They’re time frame dependent. For example, an EMA(200) on a 15 minute chart plots a vastly different curve, price-wise, to an EMA(200) on an hourly chart, and so on, simply because it uses a completely different data set. Having said this, I suspect that the most commonly used timeframes for catching large intraday moves are M15 and H1. b) Different traders use different parameters. I’ve seen everything from 5, 10, 20, 25, 30, 50, 100, 150, 200, 365 (plus Fibo numbers like 21, 34, 55, etc) used for MAs. Moreover, some traders prefer to use SMAs, some EMAs, etc. All of these plot completely different curves. For stochastics I’ve seen %K values of 5, 7, 8, 10, 13 and 14 used. I note that MACD(12,20,9) is being recommended here, which is different to the "industry standard" MACD(12,26,9). And so on. I’m not saying that the original poster is wrong . If enough "big dogs" respect and use the parameter settings that he is advocating, then these have the potential to herald reversals, for as long as this formula remains industry-accepted. TCMM, perhaps you've worked for a major bank? Could you explain how or where you obtained your information? The concept of buying dips in uptrends and selling rallies in downtrends is robust enough in principle, but could potentially be achieved using any indicator settings, or in fact without using indicators at all. Beyond this, I don’t see how curve-fit indicators on their own are capable of predicting underlying market psychology. To whatever extent mechanical systems need occasional re-optimization, supports this view. As has been wisely said elsewhere, "indicators are a symptom, not a cause". David
